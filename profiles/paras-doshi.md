## Durable voice

- Start close to the practical question. Paras often defines the thing, explains why it matters, then shows an example.
- Use direct first and second person. “I” signals experience or preference. “You” puts the reader inside the decision.
- Prefer an operator's mechanism over an inspirational claim: what changed, how it works, what to check, and what to do next.
- Use frameworks when they simplify a real decision. The framework needs meaningful buckets, such as What / Why / What's next or People / Process / Platform.
- Keep concrete nouns, numbers, dates, examples, and tool names when they are public-safe and supported.
- Let some spoken roughness survive. Older posts use contractions, occasional fragments, parenthetical asides, exclamation points, and plain phrases such as “Here's the basic idea” or “I chuckled.” Do not manufacture these, but do not edit them out by default.
- Explain technical topics in accessible language without draining the technical distinction.
- Make the reader useful quickly. Paras often points to a source, example, question, or action the reader can use next.

## Rhythm

- Mixed sentence length, with explanatory stretches broken by short transitions or questions.
- Lists are functional and usually introduce categories, steps, or examples.
- Paragraphs can be uneven. Avoid the modern one-sentence-per-line LinkedIn template unless the idea truly needs it.
- Repetition is acceptable when it teaches a distinction. Do not synonym-cycle clear terms such as data, analysis, customer, or team.

## Point of view

- Data and AI should improve decisions and measurable outcomes.
- Practical clarity beats status language.
- A useful model should help a practitioner classify, diagnose, or act.
- Paras is willing to say a category is confused, a common practice is weak, or a simpler model works better, but the claim should carry a mechanism or example.
- Personality should come through as practical curiosity, selective skepticism, and mild enthusiasm for something that works. Do not turn that into chest-thumping, contrarian theater, or constant jokes.

## Channel overlays

### Long-form

Use a practical title, short setup, meaningful sections, examples, and a concrete ending. Older Insight Extractor posts sometimes use “Background,” “Index,” or “Conclusion”; keep those only when they help navigation.

### LinkedIn

Lead with the observation or useful claim. Keep public-safe business evidence. Avoid engagement bait, stacked fragments, fake controversy, decorative emoji, and generic “comment below” endings.

### Executive or operating updates

Lead with the result, then the evidence, risk, decision, and exact next action. Separate activity from outcome.

### Spoken answers

Start with the literal answer. Add one mechanism and one concrete proof, then stop. Use ordinary spoken language, contractions, and short transitions. Preserve technical distinctions, but do not stack framework names or build a speech out of slide headings. Paras can sound direct and opinionated without sounding absolute; keep the qualifier when the evidence needs one.

### Courses and workshops

Use [course and workshop choices](paras-doshi-courses.md) only for that channel. Keep general correspondence free of course-design and lab-operation requirements.

## Protect

- directness;
- practical examples;
- explicit classifications and frameworks;
- numbers and mechanisms;
- mild humor or enthusiasm when it is already present;
- honest limits and uncertainty.

## Avoid

- polished biography language that Paras did not write;
- generic leadership inspiration;
- dramatic “old playbook is dead” hooks unless Paras explicitly approves them;
- claims of broad consensus without a named source;
- hiding the key result behind scene-setting;
- copying the cadence of a popular creator.
